SpaceX: The Psychology of the Big Bet

SpaceX just completed the largest IPO in stock market history. The stock already pulled back from its high. Here is why I am holding — and what nobody talks about when you make a long-term bet on a company trying to colonize Mars.

The Biggest IPO in History Just Happened

Five days ago, on June 12, 2026, SpaceX went public on the Nasdaq under the ticker SPCX. It was not just a big IPO. It was the largest IPO in stock market history — the company raised $75 billion in a single offering, pricing shares at $135 each.

On its first day of trading, shares closed at $160.95. By June 16, just four days later, the stock hit an all-time high of $225.64 — a 67% gain from the IPO price in less than a week. Then it pulled back. As of today it sits around $191, down nearly $35 from the top.

And that pullback — that's exactly what this article is about. Not the numbers. The psychology.

Why I Believe in SpaceX Long Term

Let me start with the honest version of my thesis. I am not buying SpaceX because of its current revenue. I am buying it because of what it is building — and because I believe the things it is building will be worth far more in 10 years than any analyst model can currently price.

Starlink alone could justify the valuation. SpaceX's satellite internet service already has millions of subscribers worldwide and is expanding into aviation, maritime, and underserved regions that traditional internet providers will never reach. It generates recurring revenue at scale — the kind of business that compounds quietly while everyone watches rocket launches.

Starship is the most important vehicle ever built. As of May 2026, SpaceX has completed 12 Starship test flights. The program has achieved booster catches, full-stack reflights, and is now testing Version 3 of the vehicle. NASA's Human Landing System contract — which depends on Starship — is still active. A second launch site is being prepared in Florida. The pace of development is unlike anything in aerospace history.

The Mars mission is not a fantasy. It is a funded, engineering-driven program with hardware already built and tested. Whether you believe humans will live on Mars in 20 years or 50 years, the company working hardest toward it is SpaceX. If they get there — or even get close — the value created will be unlike anything in modern capitalism.

"I am not buying SpaceX for next quarter. I am buying it for the next decade. Those are completely different investments requiring completely different psychology."

The Hard Part Nobody Talks About: Waiting

Here is the part that nobody in finance wants to discuss honestly. Making a long-term bet on a company is psychologically brutal. Not because the company is bad. Because of what happens between today and the future you believe in.

The stock will go down. Maybe a lot. There will be a failed Starship launch that makes headlines. There will be a quarter where Starlink subscriber growth disappoints. There will be a broader market selloff that takes everything down 30% regardless of fundamentals. And in every one of those moments, your brain will tell you to sell.

The news will be negative exactly when the stock is lowest. This is not a coincidence — it is how markets work. Bad news causes selling, selling causes lower prices, lower prices attract more negative coverage, more negative coverage causes more selling. The cycle feeds itself. And the people who sell at the bottom are usually the ones who let that cycle make their decision for them.

People around you will think you are wrong. When SpaceX stock drops 25% from where you bought it, someone in your life will say "I told you so." They will point to the stock price as evidence they were right. They were not right. They just happened to be watching the same moment you are in, and the moment looks bad. That is not analysis. That is noise.

The Principle Behind the Bet

My investing philosophy starts with a simple rule: invest in what you understand. I use SpaceX's products. I watch their launches. I understand why Starlink exists and why people pay for it. I understand why reusable rockets change the economics of space entirely. I understand what a world looks like where humans are a multi-planetary species — and I understand that the company making that happen will be one of the most valuable ever created.

That understanding is what lets me hold through the pullbacks. When the stock drops from $225 to $191, I am not panicking because I know what I own. I own a piece of the company building the infrastructure for humanity's next chapter. A 15% pullback in the first week of trading does not change that.

Morningstar currently values SpaceX at $780 billion — roughly half of what the private market valued it at. Some analysts have price targets as high as $310. Others as low as $63. That range tells you everything: nobody actually knows. The future is uncertain. But the direction — the long arc of what SpaceX is attempting — that I believe in.

How to Think About Volatility

Here is the mental framework I use when a stock I own pulls back sharply:

Ask: has anything changed about why I bought it? Not the price — the underlying reason. Did SpaceX stop launching rockets? Did Starlink lose subscribers? Did Elon Musk sell the company? If none of those things happened, then the price drop is noise. The thesis is intact.

Remember that volatility is the price you pay for returns. The stocks that go up 10x are not boring, stable, predictable companies. They are the ones that feel terrifying to hold at certain points. That discomfort is not a warning sign. It is the admission fee.

Only invest what you can afford to ignore. The fastest way to make a bad decision is to need the money back. If you need the cash in 6 months, you cannot wait 10 years for the thesis to play out. Size your position so that a 50% drop does not change your life — and then you can hold through it without emotion taking over.

My Prediction

I believe SpaceX will be worth significantly more in 10 years than it is today. Not because of a financial model. Because the company is doing things no one else is doing, at a pace no one else is matching, in industries that did not exist a decade ago and will define the next century.

The short-term is unknowable. The stock could drop to $100 before it hits $400. I do not know. Nobody does. But I know that every time Starship launches, every time a Starlink satellite goes up, every time SpaceX lands a booster and reuses it — the company gets closer to the future I am betting on.

So I am holding. Not because I am fearless. Because I did the work, I understand what I own, and I trust the thesis more than I trust the noise.

"The biggest enemy of a long-term investor is not a bad company. It is their own impatience on a bad week."
